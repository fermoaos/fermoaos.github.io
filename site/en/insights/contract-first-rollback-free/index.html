Write the contract first and rollback becomes free

For anyone about to let an agent spend money. Building a control layer for ad operations taught us that approval, audit and rollback are not bolted on afterwards; they are byproducts of what you wrote down as types on day one.

Sep 10, 2026

A ROAS of 3.2 means nothing by itself

Metrics with the same name count different things on different channels. The attribution window differs, the currency and timezone differ, whether the platform attributed it to itself or your own conversion tracking did differs, whether refunds are reflected differs, and whether tax is inside the cost differs. Without those six facts, 3.2 is just a character.

So every value carries its provenance, and code refuses to merge values whose bases differ. Not a warning, a typed error. Try to combine what cannot be combined and it fails by naming the axis that disagreed. Derived metrics are computed in exactly one function. The model does no arithmetic.

Tax treatment defaults to unknown. And a comment in the code says that two unknowns passing each other is not evidence that they match, only the absence of evidence that they differ. That distinction is what produced the next story.

The first thing the contract caught was us

Our own fixture described Naver as a single fifteen-day click window. But Naver’s total conversions are direct plus indirect, and those two columns are defined differently. A number that sums two differently-defined columns is precisely the thing this product forbids, and our data was doing it.

Verifying it took some work. The official help pages render in JavaScript and cannot be read normally, so we pulled all 771 server-rendered FAQ pages and searched them. The figure the industry widely quotes, thirty minutes for direct and fifteen days for indirect, appears in none of the 771. Its source is an agency blog post from 2012. The same sweep also showed that the tracking period is not a platform constant but a value the advertiser sets per target, and that the cost column in the bulk report quietly changed from tax-excluded to tax-included on 30 March 2026. Same channel, same column, a different meaning mid-period.

So the fix did not invent the missing numbers. We changed the window into a composite structure, left the duration of the unknown parts empty, and promoted tax to its own axis. The only way to honour the contract was to write down that we did not know.

Without the old value, rollback is a wish

Every planned action carries not only the value it will set but the value it is replacing. That one line turns rollback from a feature into a property. Undoing is replaying each action’s inverse in reverse order, and there is nothing left to design.

Rollback also does not re-ask the policy thresholds. Raising a budget needs approval; returning the world to a value it actually held before we touched it does not. That is not a bypass. We wrote down what rollback is precisely enough that there was nothing left to ask.

Approval binds to the hash of the plan

A plan’s identifier is the hash of its content. Change one character and the identifier changes and the old approval is void. There is no path for swapping the content after the approval. Denied actions do not open, either: the function that selects what to execute strips denials first, so raising privileges does not open that door.

Here is the hole we had. At first the hash covered only the list of steps, which left who created the plan and why outside the binding. A human had approved “the plan this person filed for this reason”, and what was bound was the steps alone. An internal audit axis caught it, not a user, and now all three are hashed together as a structure. We serialise a structure rather than concatenating strings so that the class of bug where two different splits produce the same input is closed at the same time.

The same day we made the action schema reject unknown fields. An unknown field that parses quietly disappears on the next serialisation, and then what the human approved on screen and what the engine executes are no longer the same bytes.

Append-only was not enough for the ledger

The audit ledger is an append-only chain linked by hashes. It is easy to assume that catches tampering, but it cannot catch truncation of the tail. Delete the last three lines and the remaining lines are still sequential and their hashes still match. The verifier goes green and the three missing entries leave no trace.

So the head of the chain is written to a side-file, and the body and the head are written inside the same lock. At verification time, a head that runs ahead means something was cut. And a case we could not adjudicate is not treated as a pass; the ledger has a separate function whose whole job is to report unknown as unknown.

Incrementality follows the same discipline. Every value is correlational by default, and only randomised assignment earns the word incremental. Geo experiments are automatically ruled infeasible because the data has no geographic dimension, and saying we cannot is more useful than pretending we can. Pre/post comparison ships with a caveat telling you not to quote the result when the parallel-trends assumption breaks. And because peeking inflates false positives from 5% to over 30%, sequential testing uses an always-valid p-value.

Why the contract comes first

In this layer, approval and audit and rollback are not features we built. They arrived because we attached provenance to metrics, a previous value to actions, and a hash to plans. In the other order they never come this cheaply. Rollback bolted on afterwards always stalls at the same question: what was the value before?

And a contract audits its author before it audits anyone else. Neither discovery in this post came from a customer. They came out of our own data and our own approval path. That is what writing the contract first buys.
